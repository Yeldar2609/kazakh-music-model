Our question: if we adapt an existing music model with Kazakh singing data, do we get music we prefer? We will tell the whole story, including the disappointing part.

The verse we asked the model to sing is about morning light over the steppe, so the sky here moves from night towards dawn as our results come in.

The challenge

Three things have to work at the same time

1

Kazakh lyrics

«Таңғы самал бетімді сипайды…»

2

Musical character

A melody that sounds recognisably Kazakh

3

Accompaniment

Instruments that support the voice convincingly

The pretrained model we start from has no Kazakh language setting.

Why is this hard? A convincing Kazakh song needs three things together.

First, the words: the lyrics must be sung in Kazakh so a listener can follow them. Second, the musical character: the melody and phrasing should feel Kazakh. Third, the accompaniment: the instruments behind the voice have to sound convincing.

The model we start from is a general music model. It has no language setting for Kazakh, so we wanted to see whether extra Kazakh data could help.

Our new data is the Kazakh Songs ASR corpus: 3,013 sung segments from 195 songs by 36 artists.

After filtering and removing exact duplicate audio, 2,977 clips remain. We split them into 2,316 for training, 334 for validation and 327 for testing. Clips from the same song always stay together, and the validation and test clips are never used for training.

One detail matters for the rest of the story: this is isolated singing with aligned lyrics. There is no band and no dombra behind the voice. It can teach words, but it does not teach arrangement.

If asked: artists can appear in more than one part, so this is not an unseen-artist test. The original recordings and transcripts are access-controlled and are not in this presentation.

How the model works

The big model stays frozen. A small adapter learns.

Here is the idea without the mathematics. A style prompt and the lyrics go in on the left, and audio comes out on the right.

The large box is the pretrained model, ACE-Step. We did not build it and we did not change it: it stays frozen. We store its numbers in a compact four-bit form so it fits on a laptop.

The gold part is what we trained: a small add-on called a LoRA adapter, with about five and a half million adjustable numbers. It sits inside the model and nudges its behaviour. Think of it as a volume knob for what the model learned from our Kazakh data. That knob becomes important later.

Why loss was not enough

The number improved. The music did not.

Earlier pilot · the metric

Held-out flow loss went down

Earlier pilot · the listening

The listener judged the music poor

So we changed
how we judge:

  • Same prompt, lyrics, seed and length for every version
  • Playback levels matched
  • Labels shuffled, identities revealed only after choosing
  • The pretrained model is allowed to win

Before the full run we did a small pilot. Its loss on held-out clips went down. Loss is the model's own error score, so on paper it looked better.

But when the project owner listened, the music was poor. A lower loss did not mean better music.

So we changed how we judge. Every version gets the same prompt, lyrics, seed and length. Loudness is matched, labels are shuffled, and identities are revealed only after choosing. The untouched pretrained model is in the line-up, and it is allowed to win.

25% strength means the adapter's influence is scaled down while generating. The 25%, 50% and 100% positions all use the same adapter, trained on all 2,316 clips — not on 25% of the data. 0% is the pretrained model alone.

1 listener · 1 prompt · 1 seed

This knob is the adapter from the diagram. Every position is a real prerecorded output of the same verse.

Round one: the listener compared the pretrained model with our checkpoints at full strength, and chose the pretrained model. At full strength our training did not win. The feedback was: better melody and accompaniment. That fits, because we trained on isolated vocals.

Round two: same adapter, turned down while generating. Press Play at 100%, then click 25% (about ten seconds; keys 1 to 4 also work). The clip keeps its place in the verse.

Twenty-five percent means a quarter of the adapter's influence. It does not mean a quarter of the data, and it is not a new training run. This time the listener chose 25%. One listener, one prompt, one seed: an encouraging sign, not proof.

Conclusion

First light — not yet daylight

What we found

  • The full run finished and was verified on a 4 GB laptop
  • Lower loss did not mean better music
  • Full strength lost to the pretrained model; 25% strength was preferred

What we cannot claim

  • One listener, one prompt, one seed, no numeric ratings
  • Pronunciation and general improvement are unverified
  • Accompaniment came from nine repeated crops

Next steps

  • More listeners, prompts and seeds
  • Native-speaker check of the lyrics and sung words
  • Richer licensed data with accompaniment

The sky has reached first light, and that is how far our evidence goes: a first sign, not daylight.

We completed and verified a full training run on a small laptop. A lower loss did not mean better music. At full strength our adapter lost to the pretrained model; at 25% it was the version the listener preferred.

The limits: one listener, one prompt, one seed, no numeric ratings, and pronunciation is unverified. Next we would ask more listeners, try more prompts and seeds, have a native speaker check the words, and add licensed data with accompaniment.

Thank you. We are happy to take questions.

Likely questions: Why an ASR dataset? It gives aligned sung text and audio, which we use for conditional generation. Why only one pass? It was a bounded local experiment. Can others reproduce it? The configuration, code, dataset revision and logs are recorded, but dataset access and similar hardware are needed.
